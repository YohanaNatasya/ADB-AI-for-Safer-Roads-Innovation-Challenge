Cluster D (low-activity/open context) is the largest single group at 41.35% of all segments — the open-road baseline with almost no POI density and low built-up share. At the other end, Cluster B (dense mixed-use urban activity center) is the most intensively built-up and POI-dense cluster, with the shortest distances to transport, offices, and commercial activity, while Clusters A and G add large volumes of commercial- and school-adjacent urban roads. Together, urban clusters A, B and G account for 28.18% of all segments and carry the built-up density that ultimately pushes their target speeds down to 30–50 km/h.

Reading the two speed indicators used below. Speed limit vs. target compares the existing/posted speed limit with the cluster's Safe-System target speed. P85 vs. target compares the observed 85th-percentile operating speed — i.e. driver behaviour — with that same target. Reported separately, they distinguish a regulatory problem from a real-world behaviour problem.

Thailand: risk is driven by the speed-limit gap

Nine of Thailand's top ten groups show Very High Excess between the existing posted speed limit and the new target speed limit — the single largest driver of Thailand's high total-risk scores, and the reason its risk distribution skews toward the top bands.

Maharashtra: mostly low-to-mid risk

Maharashtra's leading groups are dominated by Slight Excess or Align ratings on that same indicator — the existing limit is much closer to target — which keeps most segments in the low-to-middle risk bands rather than the highest ones.

How could a transport ministry or road safety agency use this?

The output is built to be used directly as a screening dashboard, not just a research dataset — it already does the work of combining variables that would otherwise sit in separate systems.

One score, many inputs already combined

The dashboard's risk score already blends multiple variables into a single number for every segment — including the gap between the existing speed limit and the new recommended target speed limit, and the gap between actual driver behaviour (P85 operating speed) and that recommended limit, alongside vulnerable-road-user exposure, intersection density, and traffic exposure. The higher the score, the more urgently that segment needs review — so an agency gets a ready-made priority order instead of having to merge raw datasets itself.

Segment-level stats, not just a score

On the dashboard itself, a user can also drill into the underlying road segment: its name, road length, road profile/context (which of the eight clusters it belongs to), and other descriptive attributes. That lets a planner move straight from "this segment is high risk" to "here is exactly where it is and what kind of road it is" — without a separate lookup step.

What would it take to apply this methodology in a country with less data availability?

Less than most road safety assessments would suggest. Every input in this methodology is drawn from open-source, globally-available datasets — principally OpenStreetMap (road network geometry, road classification, and point-of-interest tags) and the Global Human Settlement Layer, GHSL (satellite-derived built-up area and settlement context). Neither depends on a national crash database, traffic counts, or commercial data licences, and both already provide near-complete coverage of populated areas in almost every country in the world — including most low- and middle-income countries where official road safety data is thinnest. That is the core reason this approach is designed to travel beyond the two pilot geographies, rather than being bespoke to them.

Why this matters. Conventional road safety assessments are usually bottlenecked by years of crash-record compilation and field speed surveys. Because this methodology's heaviest inputs are already free, global, and pre-collected, a new country starts from a working national dataset on day one — the remaining work is calibration, not data collection.

What would still need to happen to adapt it to a lower-data-availability country:

  1. Audit local OSM completeness. Tagging density varies by country and even by city; sparsely-tagged areas may need a short, targeted mapping push (e.g. a HOT OpenStreetMap Team campaign) on priority corridors before clustering.
  2. Re-cluster on the local road network. Built-environment norms — informal markets, motorcycle-dominant streets, mixed land use — differ by country, so cluster centers should be re-derived locally rather than reusing Thailand/Maharashtra's cluster profiles.
  3. Find a low-cost proxy for observed speed (P85). This is the one input not fully covered by OSM/GHSL. Options include existing GPS probe/floating-car data from ride-hailing or logistics partners, free navigation-app speed layers, or a small manual radar-gun sample used only to validate the model rather than to cover the full network.
  4. Confirm local Safe System speed thresholds. The 30/50/70/100 km/h target bands used here reflect regional guidance; a new country should check these against its own national road safety strategy before applying them.
  5. Budget for analyst time, not new data spend. The workflow runs on free tools (QGIS, Python, open data), so the main resource requirement is GIS/analytical capacity inside the ministry or a technical partner — not data purchases or software licences.
  6. Validate with local experts, then pilot. Have local road safety engineers sanity-check cluster labels and target speeds, pilot the full scoring on one province or corridor, and confirm it matches on-the-ground experience before scaling nationally.

In short: the marginal cost of extending this methodology to a new country is mostly analytical and institutional — calibration, validation, and a modest speed-data sample — rather than a multi-year data-collection exercise.
